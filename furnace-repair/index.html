A modern furnace runs a strict startup sequence, and knowing where the sequence breaks is most of the diagnosis. The thermostat calls, the inducer motor spins up to prove draft through a pressure switch, the hot surface igniter glows, the gas valve opens, the burners light, the flame sensor confirms fire, and only then does the blower deliver heat to the house. In Gresham, OR, the failures cluster at predictable points: a flame sensor coated in oxide that can no longer prove the flame, a hot surface igniter cracked from years of thermal cycling, a pressure switch that will not close because the inducer is weak or the condensate trap on a high-efficiency model has clogged, or a control board with a burned relay. Each one stops the sequence cold, and each one mimics the others from the hallway.

Rather than guess, Gresham HVAC tests the sequence one stage at a time. We read igniter resistance, measure flame signal in microamps, check that the pressure switch responds to real inducer draft, confirm voltage at the gas valve, and test the blower and its capacitor. Because this is a fuel-burning appliance, the safety basics come with every visit: the heat exchanger gets inspected, gas connections get leak-tested, and draft gets verified. You cannot tell a furnace problem from a furnace hazard by looking at the thermostat. Before we leave, temperature rise is measured against the data plate so the fire and the airflow are balanced as the manufacturer intended.

Gresham HVAC services natural gas, propane, and electric furnaces of every major brand, from standard 80 percent units to condensing high-efficiency models with their traps, drain lines, and dual pressure switches. Our trucks carry the parts that actually fail, such as igniters, flame sensors, pressure switches, capacitors, inducer assemblies, and blower motors, so most homes have heat again before we leave. Warning Signs for Furnace Repair

The sooner you notice these red flags, the easier and cheaper the problem is to resolve. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Clicking and a glowing igniter with no burner flame
  • Air moving through the vents that never gets warm
  • Loud booms at startup caused by delayed ignition
  • Burners light, run a few seconds, then shut down repeatedly
  • Frequent on-off cycling that never reaches the thermostat setting
  • Soot, rust, or moisture near the furnace cabinet or vent pipe
  • Squeals, scrapes, or grinding noises behind the blower door
  • Yellow, wavering burner flames where they should be sharp blue

Why Professional Furnace Repair Matters

Gas, open flame, high voltage, and a flue share one cabinet in a furnace, and the most dangerous failure is the one you cannot see: a damaged heat exchanger that lets combustion gases into the air the blower sends through your home. Every morning that delayed ignition keeps booming at startup, that exchanger takes more stress. The diagnostic side has its own pitfalls. When a furnace trips its high limit, the switch itself may be bad, or the furnace may be protecting itself from a clogged filter or a failing blower. Replacing the switch when the real issue is airflow removes a safety device and leaves the actual problem in place.

Our experienced technicians treat every symptom as a question about the whole system. Flame signal gets measured rather than assumed, so a healthy sensor is never replaced while a cracked igniter or weak gas valve skates by. Draft is verified at the pressure switch and the flue. Temperature rise is checked against the data plate, which is how airflow problems get caught before they shorten the exchanger's life. And the gas side is leak-tested with instruments, not a sniff and a shrug. The result is a furnace that has been proven safe and correct under a full cycle, and an honest report if the unit is nearing the point where replacement beats another repair.

Maintenance Tips for Furnace Repair

A little routine maintenance goes a long way toward avoiding a mid-season breakdown. Put these technician-approved steps to work around your property:

  • Give the furnace room to breathe, since a crowded utility space starves the burners of air
  • Clear boxes, lint, and above all chlorine-based cleaners out of the furnace area
  • Schedule service each fall to catch worn igniters and dirty sensors before cold weather arrives
  • Test your CO detectors monthly and make sure each floor has one
  • Keep the condensate trap and drain clear on high-efficiency units to prevent lockouts
  • Notice how the furnace sounds at startup, because a quiet light-off is healthy and a boom is not
  • Change the filter monthly during heating season; it is the cheapest furnace part you will ever buy
